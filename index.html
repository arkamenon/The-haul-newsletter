Shippers face tighter capacity as trucking rates hold near record highs

According to ACT Research's June For-Hire Trucking Index, the truckload market has decisively shifted in favor of carriers, driven primarily by severe supply constraints rather than a broad surge in freight demand. Although the Freight Rate Index dipped slightly from May's record high down to 70.2, freight rates remain near historic peaks. This elevated pricing is heavily fueled by a continually shrinking driver pool, which has been exacerbated by strict new FMCSA regulations, driver school closures, and enhanced registration fraud enforcement.

At the same time, overall fleet capacity growth remains limited. Many carriers are currently holding off on new tractor purchases due to heavily depleted profit margins and the typical six-month lag it takes for spot-rate gains to translate into higher contract rates. However, looking ahead, ACT Research expects capacity expansion and fleet purchasing to accelerate in the third and fourth quarters as carriers begin to see improved contract margins and move to replace aging equipment ahead of the incoming EPA ’27 emissions standards.

$604 Million "Nuclear Verdict" Rocks Freight Brokerage Sector

A Dallas County jury has delivered a $604 million judgment against freight brokerage giant C.H. Robinson and motor carrier Lupus Superior. The ruling stems from a fatal 2021 crash and highlights the severe, escalating legal vulnerabilities third-party logistics (3PL) providers face in today's legal climate.

The Crash and the Verdict

The Incident: In March 2021, a Lupus Superior truck driven by employee Gorgonio Gonzalez plowed into several vehicles in Jackson, Mississippi. The crash killed Gonzalez and three others, and injured several more.

The Unknown Cause: Because the driver died in the collision, the exact reason for the crash—whether it was sleep deprivation, distraction, or a medical emergency—remains unknown.

The Financial Burden: While the jury distributed the blame among three parties, legal experts expect C.H. Robinson to bear the brunt of the $604 million payout because the driver is deceased and the carrier lacks the assets to cover such a massive judgment.

Week 30 spot rates cooled across van, reefer, and flatbed

Post-holiday freight demand normalized and summer produce softened, pushing spot linehaul rates lower week over week even as all three equipment types remained sharply above last year.

Dry Van — Dry van spot linehaul rates averaged $2.38 per mile in Week 30, down 2.5%, or $0.06 per mile, from the prior week as post-July 4 freight demand normalized and truckload capacity loosened. Rates ran 45.6%, or $0.74 per mile, above the same week last year and stayed within the five-year range of $1.53 to $2.90, holding below the 2022 peak.

Reefer — Reefer spot linehaul rates averaged $2.72 per mile in Week 30, a decline of 2.9%, or $0.08 per mile, week over week as summer produce volumes eased. Rates stood 39.6%, or $0.77 per mile, higher than a year ago and remained inside the five-year band of $1.82 to $3.33, short of the 2022 high.

Flatbed — Flatbed spot linehaul rates averaged $2.87 per mile in Week 30, off 2.7%, or $0.08 per mile, from the prior week amid softening industrial and construction demand. Rates climbed 40.6%, or $0.83 per mile, year over year and sat near the upper end of the five-year range of $1.84 to $3.01, about $0.14 below the five-year high.

High Rates, Low Freight: The Housing Market’s Ongoing Drag on Trucking

The single-family housing market—a major driver of flatbed and dry van freight for materials like lumber, drywall, appliances, and moving services—remains stagnant with no near-term recovery in sight.

Here is a breakdown of the current housing data and the macroeconomic factors keeping freight demand soft in this sector:

Key Housing Metrics

Pending Home Sales: Sitting 32% below the 2017–2019 average and flatlining since Q4 2022. This metric is a critical forward-looking indicator for existing home demand, and it shows no significant momentum.

New Construction Permits: Slightly more resilient, hovering 2% above 2017–2019 baseline levels. However, permits are down about 9% from 2024 and remain drastically lower than the pandemic housing boom (Q3 2020 – Q1 2022), which saw permits surge 33.5% above historical averages.
